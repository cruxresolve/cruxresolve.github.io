Designing the GhostBridge Enclosure

June 25, 2026 · Crux Resolve LLC Build Log Hardware 3D Printing

Twelve revisions. One Ender 3 Pro. A lot of failed prints and a growing pile of rejected enclosure halves on the workbench. That's the short version of how the GhostBridge enclosure came together.

The longer version is more interesting — and more useful if you're designing printed enclosures yourself.

// GhostBridge Rev 12.0 — the production enclosure

Why Design a Custom Enclosure at All

The easy path is an off-the-shelf project box. They're cheap, they're available in standard sizes, and they require no design work. We went down that path briefly and abandoned it for two reasons.

First, standard project boxes don't fit this PCB stack well. The combination of the microcontroller module, the RS232 level shifter, and the DB9 connector creates an unusual footprint that doesn't map cleanly to off-the-shelf sizes. You end up with either a box that's too big with components rattling around inside, or too small with clearance issues around the connectors.

Second, a custom enclosure is a product decision as much as a functional one. Something with a debossed logo and rubber feet looks like a finished product. Something in a generic grey project box looks like a prototype. For GhostBridge, the enclosure is part of the product.

The Design Tool: OpenSCAD

The enclosure is designed in OpenSCAD — a code-based 3D modeling tool where geometry is defined programmatically rather than by pushing and pulling surfaces in a GUI editor. Every dimension is a variable. Every feature is a calculation.

This approach pays off when you're iterating. Changing the wall thickness, adjusting the connector clearance, moving the screw holes — you change a number at the top of the file and regenerate. No redrawing. No realigning. The model updates everywhere that number was used.

Print settings throughout development: 0.4mm nozzle, 0.2mm layer height, 4 perimeters, PLA. These stayed consistent across all twelve revisions so dimensional changes were the only variable between prints.

What Each Revision Fixed

Most revisions were small — a fraction of a millimeter here, a structural reinforcement there. But each one addressed something real.

Rev What Changed
1–3Initial fit and sizing. Getting the PCB to sit flat with correct standoff height and connector alignment.
4–5Connector opening geometry. The DB9 slot needed clearance on all sides for the metal shell without being sloppy.
6–7Lid fit. The locating lip system replaced a simple flat-top approach. First lid that snapped together properly.
8–9Screw retention. Added M3 rail system so the lid is mechanically retained on both ends, not just press-fit.
10DB9 closure reinforcement. Increased the lid plate overlap at the DB9 end from 0.5mm to 1.5mm for a stronger PLA joint.
11USB-C closure. Reinforced the lid closure at the USB-C end with a tapered root — thicker at the base, correct clearance at the connector face.
12Screw rail geometry. Shortened and centered all four screw support rails to exactly 10mm. Added anti-flex ribs that stop inside the locating lip instead of piercing through it.

The Hardest Problem: Connector Clearance

The DB9 connector is the most mechanically demanding feature. It's a large connector relative to the enclosure — the slot for it spans most of the short end wall. The metal shell of the connector needs clearance on all sides to seat fully, but too much clearance and it looks sloppy and lets in debris.

The other complication is that the DB9 connector extends into the space where the lid's locating lip would normally sit. The lip has to be cut away at the DB9 end, which reduces the structural contribution of that section of the lid. The Rev 10 closure reinforcement was specifically designed to compensate for that — fusing more of the lid plate into the DB9 end wall to recover the lost rigidity.

The USB-C End

The USB-C connector presented a different challenge. Unlike the DB9, it's a small connector that projects only about 1mm beyond the edge of the PCB. A standard 3mm wall thickness would be too thick for a cable to seat fully — the cable shell would hit the wall before the connector bottomed out.

The solution is a recessed section of wall around the USB-C opening — locally 1mm thick in the connector zone, with the full 3mm wall everywhere else. Rev 11 added a tapered root to this closure on the lid side so the locally thin section is reinforced structurally while preserving the clearance geometry.

The Lid System

The lid uses a locating lip — a 1mm perimeter ring that drops 2mm into the base cavity. It registers the lid position precisely and provides resistance to lateral movement. The M3 screws on both short ends then clamp the lid down and prevent it from lifting.

The screw rails are printed as part of the lid and extend downward into the base cavity. M3 pilot holes are drilled directly into PLA — the thread engagement is enough for assembly and disassembly without inserts. Using clearance holes in the base wall and threaded pilots in the lid rails means the screws pull the lid down as they're tightened, which seats the locating lip cleanly.

Anti-Flex Ribs

With the screw rails at both ends and the locating lip around the perimeter, the center of the lid was the remaining weak point. Without support, the lid plate could flex slightly under pressure. Two internal ribs span the width of the lid in the center section, running from locating lip to locating lip. Rev 12 shortened these ribs so they stop just inside the lip rather than piercing through the outer face — removing a stress concentration point that had caused a small number of prints to crack along the rib endpoint.

Branding

The lid exterior has two debossed features: the ghost logo and the GhostTune wordmark. Debossing — cutting into the surface rather than raising above it — works better than embossing on FDM prints because it doesn't require bridging or overhangs. The features are shallow (0.35mm depth) and narrow (1.2mm line width), which keeps them crisp at 0.2mm layer height without creating weak spots in the lid plate.

Going Forward

Rev 12 is the production enclosure for the first batch of GhostBridge units. Future production batches will move from FDM printing on the Ender 3 Pro to manufactured enclosures — the OpenSCAD design exports clean geometry that translates directly to other production methods.

The OpenSCAD source file will be made available for anyone who wants to inspect the geometry, verify the dimensions, or adapt the design for their own purposes.
